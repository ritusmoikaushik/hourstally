Why payroll wants decimal, and why 8:30 is not 8.30

A clock counts sixty minutes to the hour. A pay calculation multiplies hours by a rate, and multiplication only works in base ten. So payroll systems, and almost every timekeeping export, store time as a decimal fraction of an hour: thirty minutes is half an hour, so 8:30 becomes 8.50. It is not 8.30. That mistake, made once a week for a year, underpays a worker by about twenty minutes a week — seventeen hours over the year.

The conversion is minutes divided by sixty. Forty-five minutes is 45 ÷ 60 = 0.75. Five minutes is 5 ÷ 60 = 0.0833, which rounds to 0.08. Twenty minutes is 0.33, forty is 0.67. The awkward ones are anything not divisible by three: seven minutes is 0.1167, which is 0.12 at two places. The chart at the bottom of this page has all sixty.

Two decimal places is the usual precision — but check

Most payroll systems, ADP, Paychex, QuickBooks, Gusto and the like, accept two decimal places and round internally. Two places means the largest possible error on any single entry is a third of a minute, which over two weeks adds up to less than five minutes and in practice cancels out. Some systems accept four places. If yours does, the note under the converter shows the four-place figure too.

What you should not do is round to one place. 7:45 as 7.8 gives the worker an extra three minutes; 7:20 as 7.3 takes away two. It looks tidy and it is wrong in both directions.

Quarter-hour and tenth-hour rounding

Rounding is a different thing from conversion, and the two get confused. Conversion changes the format. Rounding changes the number.

Quarter-hour rounding snaps a time to the nearest 0.25 — so 7:52 becomes 7.75 and 7:53 becomes 8.00, because 7:53 is closer to 8:00 than to 7:45. This is the common employer practice in the United States and it is lawful under the Fair Labor Standards Act only if it rounds both ways and averages out over time. A system that rounds clock-ins up and clock-outs down is not rounding, it is taking wages.

Tenth-hour rounding snaps to the nearest six minutes — 0.1 of an hour. It is common in legal billing, healthcare and some government payroll. 7:52 becomes 7.9; 7:57 becomes 8.0.

The rounding selector at the top applies to both the single converter and the column converter, so you can see your exact hours and your rounded hours side by side and know what the rounding is costing or giving you.

Going the other way

A pay stub shows 76.42 hours for the two weeks and you want to know what that is on a clock. Type it in the decimal box: 0.42 of an hour is 25 minutes, so 76.42 is 76:25. If your own record says 76:35, the difference is ten minutes, and now you have a specific number to raise rather than a feeling that something is off.

Questions people ask

Is 7.5 hours seven and a half hours or seven hours five minutes?

Seven and a half. A decimal hour is a fraction of an hour, so .5 is half an hour, thirty minutes. Seven hours five minutes would be 7.08. When someone writes 7.5 meaning 7:05 the pay stub is wrong by twenty-five minutes.

What is 0.83 hours in minutes?

Fifty minutes. Multiply the decimal part by sixty: 0.83 × 60 = 49.8, which is fifty minutes once rounded. The chart shows 50 minutes as 0.83 exactly.

Why does my payroll show 8.08 when I worked 8:05?

Because five minutes is 0.0833 of an hour and the system shows two decimal places. Nothing is missing. 8.08 hours is 8 hours and 4.8 minutes, which rounds back to 8:05.

Can I convert negative time, for a deduction?

Yes. Type -0:30 and it converts to -0.50. Useful for correcting a previous period on a timesheet.

Does the chart change with the rounding setting?

No. The chart always shows all three columns — exact, quarter-hour and tenth-hour — so it is complete when printed. The rounding setting applies to the converters above it.
